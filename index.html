    MODULE 01 · 4 HOURS

    Introduction to Software Project Management

    Define what success looks like, decide whether the project belongs in the portfolio, then turn the idea into an approved plan.

    Project Definition

    [Extra from Syllabus sheet]

    A project is temporary work undertaken to deliver a unique product, service, or result. Building an online examination system for a university is a project. Running its help desk every semester is ongoing operations.

    Importance of Software Project Management

    [Extra from Syllabus sheet]

    Software Project Management is the planning, organization, direction, monitoring, and control of people and work to deliver software within agreed scope, time, cost, and quality constraints.

    Software is intangible, requirements can evolve, components interact, and delivery depends heavily on people and specialized knowledge. A working demo, accepted milestone, or passing test provides more evidence of progress than hours spent. Software Project Management helps define scope and goals, estimate time, cost and effort, allocate people and tools, prepare risk responses, coordinate stakeholders, and manage delivery against a baseline.

    Use a specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound objective. “Release online exams for 500 simultaneous students by 30 September and pass a 500-user load test with 95% of responses under 2 seconds.” Scope is what work is included. A deliverable is what you hand over. An objective is the result or target you aim to achieve.

    [Added for Understanding] Project types. Classify by the work driving the plan. A product-driven project starts with a defined deliverable, such as a specified results portal. An objective-driven project starts with a result, such as reducing exam administration time, and evaluates software and process alternatives. New development, enhancement and migration are also useful categories when estimating based on comparable projects.

    Project Portfolio Management

    [Extra from Syllabus sheet]

    Project Portfolio Management selects, prioritizes, funds, balances, and reviews projects and programs together to support organizational strategy under shared constraints. A program coordinates related projects to realize shared benefits. A portfolio may group unrelated projects competing for the same budget, people, or infrastructure.

    1. Collect proposals and business cases. Screen for mandatory obligations, strategic fit, and basic feasibility.
    2. Compare value, risk, dependencies, urgency, and scarce resources. Use financial metrics and a weighted score where appropriate.
    3. Select and sequence a mix of investments. Assign funding and capacity, settle conflicts, and document the rationale.
    4. Review benefits, resources, and risks periodically. Continue, redirect, defer, or stop investments as conditions change.

    Example. An exam platform, a student analytics dashboard, and a regulatory reporting upgrade all need the same database engineer. Portfolio management sets the order and funding. The manager of the selected exam project plans and controls that project’s delivery.

    Overview of Project Planning

    Project planning defines objectives, scope, schedule, budget and resources. It identifies the activities required for successful project completion and serves as a roadmap.

    Objectives · 6 points

    • Define project goals & scope.
    • Estimate time, cost & effort.
    • Allocate resources effectively.
    • Identify risks & prepare mitigation plan.
    • Ensure timely delivery within budget.
    • Improve coordination among team members.

    Importance / Benefits · 5 points

    • Reduce uncertainty & project risk.
    • Improve communication among stakeholders.
    • Ensure efficient use of resources.
    • Help monitor & control project progress.
    • Increase the chance of project success.
    Project plan and baseline explanation · [Added for Understanding]

    Project planning is the process of deciding what to deliver, when, by whom, with which resources, and within which cost. Meet stakeholders, agree and prioritize goals, define deliverables, create a schedule, assess issues and risks, and present the plan for approval. The approved baseline is the reference for measuring later performance. The schedule forecast can evolve as fresh information arrives.

    The plan acts as a roadmap. It reduces uncertainty, improves stakeholder communication and team coordination, and shows whether resources can support delivery on time and within budget. Later it provides the reference for measuring progress and taking action.

    Strategic Assessment and Technical Assessment

    Strategic Assessment

    Strategic assessment evaluates whether a software project supports the organization’s business strategy and contributes to its long-term objectives.

    Why? · 4 points

    • Ensure the project aligns with business goals.
    • Evaluate the business value.
    • Prioritize projects based on organizational needs.
    • Support investment decisions.

    Key considerations. Business objectives, market demands, competitive advantages, cost-benefit analysis, risks & opportunities, and resource availability.

    Outcome. Decision to approve, defer or reject the project.

    Strategic assessment explanation · [Added for Understanding]

    Strategic assessment evaluates long-term fit and business value. Use it to prioritize proposals, compare benefits and risks, and decide which projects warrant investment. Check business objectives, market demand, competitive advantage, cost-benefit case, resources and opportunities. The decision may be to approve, defer or reject.

    Technical Assessment

    Technical assessment evaluates whether a software project can be developed successfully using available technology, skills and resources.

    Checks · 7 points

    • Technical feasibility.
    • Hardware & software requirements.
    • Development tools.
    • Team expertise.
    • System architecture.
    • Performance, security & scalability.
    • Integration with existing systems.

    Output. Decision on the project’s technical feasibility and an appropriate technology stack.

    Technical assessment explanation · [Added for Understanding]

    Technical assessment asks whether the proposed system can be built and operated in the available environment. Check architecture, hardware, software, tools, team expertise, integration, performance, scalability and security. Decide whether the system is feasible and identify the gaps or a suitable technology stack. Use a proof of concept or integration check when a key assumption is uncertain.

    Example · Artificial Intelligence–Based Healthcare Management System.

    Strategic Assessment. Supports the digital healthcare strategy and meets market demand → provides a positive return on investment.

    Technical Assessment. Check artificial intelligence expertise, availability of cloud infrastructure and machine-learning tools, and whether the system can meet performance, security and scalability requirements.

    Cost Benefit Analysis

    Cost Benefit Analysis compares a project’s expected costs with its anticipated benefits to determine whether the project is economically worthwhile.

    Objectives · 4 points

    • Evaluate project feasibility.
    • Justify project investment.
    • Compare alternative solutions.
    • Maximize Return on Investment.

    Types of costs · 4 points

    • Development cost. Software development, testing and deployment.
    • Operational cost. Maintenance, support and hosting.
    • Training cost. User and staff training.
    • Infrastructure cost. Hardware & software, software licenses and cloud services.

    Benefits · 6 points

    • Increased productivity.
    • Reduced operational cost.
    • Increased customer satisfaction.
    • Increased revenue.
    • Better decision making.
    • Competitive advantages.

    Advantages · 4 points

    • Support informed decision making.
    • Help prioritize projects.
    • Improve resource allocation.
    • Identify financial risk early.
    Cost benefit analysis explanations · [Added for Understanding]
    1. Identify development, operating, training and infrastructure costs, including their timing.
    2. Estimate benefits, including annual gains and one-off proceeds such as salvage.
    3. Convert comparable impacts to money and document assumptions for intangible benefits.
    4. Compare on a common time and price basis. Benefits above costs pass the stated test, equality is break-even, and lower benefits call for reconsideration or rejection.
    5. Use the result with strategic fit, technical feasibility and risk to justify the investment, compare alternatives and allocate funds.

    Cash Flow Forecasting

    Cash Flow Forecasting estimates the cash inflow and outflow over a specific period to ensure sufficient funds are available.

    Objectives · 5 points

    • Estimate future cash requirements.
    • Maintain project liquidity.
    • Plan the budget effectively.
    • Avoid cash shortages.
    • Support financial planning.

    Cash inflow. Client payment, project funding, product sales and service revenue.

    Cash outflow. Employee salary, software license, hardware purchase, cloud services, maintenance expenses and training cost.

    Benefits · 5 points

    • Improve financial control.
    • Identify funding needs early.
    • Reduce financial risks.
    • Ensure timely project execution.
    • Support investment decisions.
    Timing and cash balance explanation · [Added for Understanding]

    Cash Flow Forecasting estimates actual timing of receipts and payments to protect liquidity. Inflows include client payments, funding, sales, and service receipts. Outflows include salaries, licenses, hardware, cloud, maintenance and training. Estimate receipts and payments per period, record credit collections when expected to arrive, calculate net flow and closing balance, then use that balance as the next opening balance. Review variances and update the forecast as due dates or costs change. This supports budgeting and investment decisions, identifies funding needs early, reduces cash shortage risk, and keeps work funded when required.

    Net cash flow = cash receipts − cash payments
    Closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow

    Cost Benefit Analysis asks whether the value justifies the investment. Cash Flow Forecasting asks whether cash is available when bills fall due. A profitable project can still face a cash shortage before customers pay.

    Cost Benefit Evaluation Techniques

    Objectives · 4 points

    • Determine whether the benefits of a project exceed its cost.
    • Compare alternative projects.
    • Support effective investment decisions.
    • Ensure efficient use of resources.
    Time value of money · [Added for Understanding]

    [Added for Understanding] Time value of money. At a 10% discount rate, ₹110 received one year from now has a present value of ₹110 ÷ 1.10 = ₹100 today. Year 2 has two periods of discounting. Cash spent now is already at time 0 and has factor 1. Apply the specified rate using the same period as the cash flows.

    Risk Evaluation

    Risk evaluation analyzes identified uncertainties, estimates likelihood and impact on scope, schedule, cost, quality and benefits, and prioritizes responses. Typical categories are technical (defects, new technology, integration), project (delay, staffing, budget), business (market change, customer withdrawal, regulation), and external (pandemic, disaster, policy). Look for cost estimating errors, unclear customer requirements, delay, resource shortage, and external constraints. Compare risks using a common scale and develop responses for those beyond tolerance.

    40% × ₹50,000 = ₹20,000 expected loss

    • Reduce project uncertainty.

    Sequencing and Scheduling Activities

    Dependency types, scheduling practice and common problems · [Added for Understanding]
    1. Identify activities from the Work Breakdown Structure, such as requirements, design, coding, testing, and deployment.
    2. Estimate duration, such as 2 weeks for requirements, 8 for coding, and 4 for testing. Record assumptions and availability.
    3. Determine predecessors and dependency type. In a simple software sequence, design follows requirements, coding follows design, and testing follows coding. Finish-to-start links a successor’s start to its predecessor’s finish. Other types are start-to-start, finish-to-finish, and start-to-finish. A lag adds waiting time and a lead allows overlap.
    4. Allocate developers, testers, database engineers and user interface designers. Resolve overbooking and account for calendars.
    5. Build the schedule with a Gantt chart, network diagram, Critical Path Method or Program Evaluation and Review Technique. Check deadlines, milestones and resource feasibility before baselining.
    6. Compare planned and actual dates regularly, update forecasts, and review changes to the baseline through change control.

    Common problems. Missing dependencies, optimistic estimates, overloaded resources, holiday omissions, late approvals, and stale status. Give each task an owner, update remaining duration, and record calendar, resource and constraint assumptions. Make dependencies visible and inspect critical and near-critical sequences after changes.

    Network Planning Models

    A Gantt chart places activities on a calendar. A network shows their precedence, helps build and monitor the schedule, and exposes the effect of delays. Activity on Node puts activities in nodes, with arrows giving dependencies. Activity on Arrow puts activities on arrows and events in nodes. The Critical Path Method normally uses single duration estimates to identify the longest-duration path, float and schedule compression options. The Program Evaluation and Review Technique uses three duration estimates to model uncertainty. Both use precedence and critical path analysis.

    Critical Path Method versus Program Evaluation and Review Technique. Critical Path Method uses specified durations, calculates critical paths and float, and supports time–cost trade-offs. The Program Evaluation and Review Technique estimates duration under uncertainty from optimistic O, most likely M, and pessimistic P estimates. Its mean is (O + 4M + P) ÷ 6 and variance is [(P − O) ÷ 6]2. Module 4 works through the probability calculation.

    Elapsed time and dependency convention · [Added for Understanding]

    [Added for Understanding] Read one dependency at a time. A predecessor must satisfy the stated link before its successor can proceed. In this example every link is finish-to-start with zero lag. If a task starts at elapsed time 3 and runs for 5 days, it finishes at time 8. Count the duration as a difference between time boundaries.

    Forward Pass

    Start at time 0 for activities with no predecessors. Visit tasks in precedence order. At a join, use the largest predecessor finish because every prerequisite must be finished. The largest finish among terminal activities is the earliest project finish.

    Earliest start = maximum predecessor earliest finish
    Earliest finish = earliest start + duration

    Backward Pass

    Start from the project finish and visit tasks in reverse precedence order. For an unconstrained Critical Path Method calculation, seed terminal activities with the earliest project finish. At a branch, the earliest allowable start among successors sets the latest permissible finish. Use a specified project deadline as the seed when the question requires it.

    Latest finish = minimum successor latest start
    Latest start = latest finish − duration

    Activity Float

    Total float is the delay a task can absorb before delaying the project finish under the stated schedule. Free float is the delay it can absorb before shifting any successor’s earliest start.

    Total float = latest start − earliest start = latest finish − earliest finish
    Free float = minimum successor earliest start − earliest finish
    For a terminal activity, use the earliest project finish in the free float formula.

    In the usual unconstrained Critical Path Method exercise, the longest precedence path is critical and its activities have zero total float. There can be more than one such path. A deadline earlier than the calculated finish can produce negative total float, which measures how much the stated schedule misses the deadline along affected paths.

    Activity on Arrow Networks

    Activity on Arrow is a scheduling technique in which activities are represented by arrows and events by nodes.

    Dummy activities and the Activity on Arrow calculation method · [Added for Understanding]

    An event is a zero-duration milestone reached after all incoming work. An arrow has activity and duration. Label all arrows clearly and number events in increasing precedence order. Use common start and finish events in the textbook network. A zero-duration dummy activity shows a logical dependency or keeps activities distinguishable when their head and tail events would otherwise coincide. Example: A and B start together, C depends only on A, and D depends on A and B. Use separate A and B end events, start C at A’s end, and add a dummy from A’s end to B’s end to make D wait for both.

    Solving an Activity on Arrow network. Set the start event to zero and compute each earliest event time using every incoming arrow. Set the latest finish event to the required finish, or the calculated earliest finish if the question supplies none. Move backward, checking every outgoing arrow. Then convert event times into each arrow’s dates and float.

    Earliest event E(j) = maximum over incoming arrows of [E(i) + d(i,j)]
    Latest event L(i) = minimum over outgoing arrows of [L(j) − d(i,j)]
    For arrow i → j: earliest start = E(i), earliest finish = E(i) + d, latest finish = L(j), latest start = L(j) − d
    Total float = L(j) − E(i) − d · free float = E(j) − E(i) − d

    Risk Management

    Identify uncertainty before it becomes an issue, plan a response, and estimate how uncertain activity times affect the project deadline.

    Nature of Risk

    A hazard is a source or condition with potential for harm. A risk is an uncertain event or condition affecting objectives. Threats have adverse effects and opportunities can create favorable effects. An issue is a problem already present. Example: a single developer understands the payment integration (hazard), that developer might leave before release (risk), and a resignation has been submitted (issue).

    Types of Risk

    Types of Risk · 4 types

    1. Technical ← Software defects, new technology, integration problems.
    2. Project Risk ← Schedule delay, budget overrun, resource shortage.
    3. Business Risk ← Market changes, customer withdrawal, regulatory changes.
    4. External Risk ← Natural disasters, pandemic, government policies.
    Notes on overlapping classifications · [Added for Understanding]

    Classify risks as technical (defects, unstable technology, integration, data loss), project/management (schedule, budget, staffing), business (market, sponsorship, customer withdrawal), and external (disaster, regulation, supplier or policy change). Categories can overlap. State the cause, uncertain event, and effect.

    Managing Risk

    Start with identifying uncertainty, then analyze likelihood and impact, evaluate priority, assign actions, and monitor/control results. Review the resulting risk register at regular checkpoints and when scope, staffing, dependencies or assumptions change. Residual risk remains after a response. Secondary risk arises because of a response.

    For a positive opportunity, enhance its chance or impact, share it with a capable partner, exploit it by acting to make it happen, or accept it and watch for an opportunity to act. The threat responses below address likely losses.

    Program Evaluation and Review Technique

    The Program Evaluation and Review Technique is a network-based project management technique for planning, scheduling, monitoring and control when activity durations are uncertain.

    Objectives · 4 points

    • Estimate the expected time required to complete the project.
    • Identify activities critical to project completion.
    • Determine the probability of completing the project within a specified time.
    • Identify activities whose delays may affect the overall project.

    Characteristics · 6 points

    1. Probabilistic technique. Activity times are uncertain.
    2. Network based. Activities and dependencies are represented in a project network.
    3. Event oriented. Focuses on events and milestones.
    4. Time focused. Focuses on completion time.
    5. Useful for uncertain projects. For example, research and development projects.
    6. Identifies critical activities. Determines the critical path.

    Estimates · 3 times

    • Optimistic time (to). Minimum possible time under favorable conditions.
    • Most likely time (tm). Normal completion time.
    • Pessimistic time (tp). Maximum expected time under unfavorable conditions.
    How the uncertain estimates are used · [Added for Understanding]

    The Program Evaluation and Review Technique is a probabilistic, time-focused network technique for planning, scheduling, monitoring and control when activity durations are uncertain, such as research and development. Its traditional event orientation makes milestones visible. It estimates completion time, identifies critical activities and the delays that can affect the finish, and estimates the chance of a specified deadline. Estimate optimistic time O under favorable conditions, most likely M under normal conditions, and pessimistic P under unfavorable conditions, with O ≤ M ≤ P. The notes also write these as to, tm and tp. The most likely time is a typical estimate. The expected time in the Program Evaluation and Review Technique weights this estimate together with both extremes. Identify activities and their dependencies, estimate O, M and P for each, compute expected time and variance, then draw the network. Run the forward/backward passes on the expected durations. Find the longest expected-duration path, then estimate deadline probability using that path.

    Expected activity time tₑ = (O + 4M + P) ÷ 6
    Activity standard deviation σ = (P − O) ÷ 6
    Activity variance σ² = [(P − O) ÷ 6]²
    Expected critical-path time TE = μ = Σ tₑ
    Path variance = Σ σ² (independent activities)
    Path standard deviation = √(path variance)
    z = (deadline − μ) ÷ path standard deviation · Completion probability ≈ Φ(z)

    The textbook estimate treats the expected critical path as controlling completion and approximates its duration as normal. Add variances on that path and take the square root once. Close parallel paths, switching critical paths, correlated durations and small samples weaken this approximation. For zero variance, the path is deterministic under the stated model.

    Worked Program Evaluation and Review Technique example · Can we finish in 17 days?

    A → B → (C and D in parallel) → E. Assume independent activity times. The finish of both C and D is required for E.

    For activity B, tₑ = (2 + 4 × 4 + 6) ÷ 6 = 4 days. Its variance is [(6 − 2) ÷ 6]2 = 4/9 days². Repeat for every activity. A–B–C–E has mean 2 + 4 + 5 + 4 = 15 days. A–B–D–E takes 12 expected days. The expected critical path is A–B–C–E. Its variance is (1+4+4+4)/9 = 13/9 days². Standard deviation ≈ 1.2019 days. z = (17−15)/1.2019 ≈ 1.664. From the standard normal cumulative distribution function, Φ(z) ≈ 95.2%. This is the textbook approximation for finishing by day 17.

    Practice · Interpret a negative z-score

    Using the same path, how likely is completion by day 14?

    Answer. z = (14−15)/1.2019 ≈ −0.832. The left-tail probability Φ(−0.832) ≈ 20.3%. With a table listing only positive z, use 1 − Φ(0.832).

The chance of finishing later is 1 − Φ(z). Keep variance in days² until you take the square root, so the numerator and denominator of z are both in days.

    Monitoring and Control

    Measure what has actually been delivered, diagnose deviations, control approved changes and manage supplier commitments through acceptance.

    Creating Framework

    [Extra from Syllabus sheet] Formal monitoring design.

    Monitoring observes and reports actual performance. Control uses the evidence to take corrective action. A framework defines what to measure, who owns it, how and when to collect it, which approved baseline to use, and when a variance requires escalation. Agree reporting periods, thresholds, milestone criteria and decision authority. For example, for a milestone due at week 6, the quality assurance lead provides accepted test counts each Friday. A threatened critical milestone is escalated immediately, with expected impact and action owner.

    Collecting the Data

    [Extra from Syllabus sheet]

    Collect actual starts and finishes, remaining duration/effort, approved completion, delivered units, defects, risks, resource usage, invoices and actual cost as of the same status date. Sources include timesheets, task trackers, acceptance records, test reports, invoices and team status. Validate completeness, dates, units, accounting cutoff and consistency. Use objective completion rules. Under 0/100, a short task earns all its budgeted value on completion. Under 50/50, it earns half on start and half on completion. For larger work packages, allocate value to weighted milestones and earn each portion only when its criteria are met.

    See the Project Control Cycle under Getting the Project Back to Target.

    [Added for Understanding] Baseline, actual and forecast. The baseline says when you agreed to finish. The actual records events that have happened. The forecast says when you now expect to finish. A status date aligns all three readings, so a late invoice or an early status update does not distort the comparison.

    Cost Monitoring

    [Extra from Syllabus sheet]

    Cost monitoring compares actual, committed and forecast expenditure with time-phased budget by work package. Record incurred labor and supplier costs consistently even if payment timing differs. Investigate rate, hours, procurement, rework and scope drivers. Costs below the planned spend can result from delayed work. Earned Value incorporates how much work was accomplished.

    Separate costs incurred, commitments (such as an approved order) and payments already made. Example: an invoice due next month can represent work performed today. Keep the cash forecast and cost report aligned to their respective timing rules.

    Earned Value Management

    [Extra from Syllabus sheet]

    Earned value relates the budgeted value of completed work to both what was planned and what was spent. All three measures use the same scope and status date. Use the approved budget weights, since completing two low-cost tasks out of five need not earn 40% of the budget.

    Worked Earned Value Management example · Ahead or behind?

    At week 5, budget at completion = ₹10 lakh, 50% scheduled, 40% earned on a budget-weighted basis, actual cost = ₹5 lakh. Planned value = ₹5 lakh, earned value = ₹4 lakh. Cost variance = 4 − 5 = −₹1 lakh. Schedule variance = 4 − 5 = −₹1 lakh. Cost performance index = 0.8 and schedule performance index = 0.8. We earn ₹0.80 of budgeted value per ₹1 spent. The budgeted value earned is 80% of the budgeted value due by now.

    If cost performance index continues, estimate at completion = 10/0.8 = ₹12.5 lakh, estimate to complete = 7.5 lakh, variance at completion = −2.5 lakh. If the variance is a one-off and remaining work proceeds at budgeted rate, estimate at completion = 5 + (10 − 4) = ₹11 lakh. State the assumption before selecting a formula.

    Practice · Why low spending can mislead

    Planned value is ₹6 lakh, earned value ₹4 lakh, actual cost ₹5 lakh. Are we under budget for the work done?

    Answer. Actual cost is below planned spending, but cost variance = ₹4 lakh − ₹5 lakh = −₹1 lakh. The completed work cost more than budgeted. Schedule variance = ₹4 lakh − ₹6 lakh = −₹2 lakh. Compare calendar dates separately to estimate delay.

    Prioritizing Monitoring

    Prioritizing monitoring identifies the most important activities, deliverables, risks and performance areas that require closer and continuous monitoring.

    Areas. Critical activities, high-risk activities, cost, schedule, quality, resources, milestones and change.

    Factors used. Criticality, risk level, cost impact, schedule impact, quality impact, dependencies and project visibility.

    Prioritizing monitoring in practice · [Added for Understanding]

    Monitor critical/near-critical activities, high-risk work, expensive work, resource bottlenecks, milestones, quality gates, dependencies and approved changes most closely.     Applying corrective actions in practice · [Added for Understanding]

    2. Discover the root cause by checking the activities driving the delay or overrun.

    Acceptance explanation and payment implications · [Added for Understanding]
    1. Define criteria. Set specific, measurable conditions during contract preparation.

    5. Identify deficiencies. List deviations, defects and incomplete requirements.

    Provisional acceptance is temporary, often pending minor remaining items. Partial acceptance covers specified components or milestones. Final acceptance confirms that the full delivery meets the acceptance requirements. Conditional acceptance depends on stated conditions being fulfilled. The agreement defines their effects, including milestone payments and any ongoing warranty or support.

    Practice · Acceptance and control in a supplier case

    3 · Object Points and the Constructive Cost ModelObject points = Σ(count × weight).
    New object points = object points × (1 − reuse fraction).
    Effort in person-months = new object points ÷ productivity rate.
    Basic Constructive Cost Model effort = a × (thousands of lines of code)b.
    Development time in months = c × (effort in person-months)d.
    4 · Risk and the Program Evaluation and Review TechniqueExposure = probability × impact. Expected activity time = (optimistic time + 4 × most likely time + pessimistic time) ÷ 6. Activity variance = [(pessimistic time − optimistic time) ÷ 6]².
    Path standard deviation = √(sum of activity variances), assuming independence. z = (deadline − expected path duration) ÷ path standard deviation. Completion probability ≈ Φ(z).
    5 · Earned ValueCost variance = earned value − actual cost.
    Schedule variance = earned value − planned value.
    Cost performance index = earned value ÷ actual cost.
    Schedule performance index = earned value ÷ planned value.
    If cost efficiency continues, estimate at completion = budget at completion ÷ cost performance index.
    Estimate to complete = estimate at completion − actual cost.
    Variance at completion = budget at completion − estimate at completion.
    6 · Job designMotivating potential score = [(skill variety + task identity + task significance)/3] × autonomy × feedback.

    Closed-book exam drills

    1 · Explain the planning sequence from objectives to approval

    Check your answer against the eight Stepwise Project Planning Activities. Give one output for each step and include approval responsibilities.

    2 · Interpret a baseline shift and a negative float

    A forecast may slip while the approved finish date stays the same. In a backward pass seeded with that earlier baseline deadline, negative float expresses schedule shortfall under the modeled dependencies.

    3 · Compute Object Points and effort quickly

    Five simple screens, two medium reports, one third-generation language component. At 20% reuse and a productivity rate of 10, object points = 5×1 + 2×5 + 1×10 = 25, new object points = 20, effort = 2 person-months.

    4 · Analyze expected exposure and residual risk

    Original exposure: 30% × ₹2 lakh = ₹60,000. If mitigation reduces probability to 10% with impact unchanged, residual exposure = ₹20,000. The expected loss decreases by ₹40,000 before mitigation cost.

    5 · Compute Earned Value Management metrics using planned value of 60, earned value of 50, and actual cost of 45

    Schedule variance = −10, cost variance = +5, schedule performance index ≈ 0.83 and cost performance index ≈ 1.11. Behind scheduled value, under budget for work accomplished. Units match the inputs. Check the critical path to infer calendar impact.

    6 · Diagnose a team problem using two Organizational Behavior levels

    Example: an individual has low confidence after repeated criticism, and the group has a norm of blaming quality assurance staff. Train and support the person and change group norms through shared defect triage and constructive reviews.

    7 · Draft a five-point closeout answer

    Record decision and acceptance, hand over operations, settle contracts and accounts, release staff/archive records, and conduct review and assign benefit owners.
